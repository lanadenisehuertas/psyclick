P20 [Heading 1]: PROJECT OVERVIEW
P21 [Normal]: PsyClick is a standalone, local-first clinical decision support system designed to assist mental health professionals in detecting single-session psychomotor disturbances through high-resolution keystroke and cursor dynamics. During a structured 10- to 15-minute assessment, the system records timestamped keyboard and mouse events, applies hardware normalization, constructs an individualized within-session Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) baseline, and evaluates eight psychomotor biomarkers using Hotelling’s T² multivariate anomaly detection. Feature Contribution Analysis decomposes the anomaly into a Psychomotor Slowing Index (PSI) and Psychomotor Agitation Index (PAI), while a fuzzy logic classifier and heuristic decision tree convert the statistical profile into Green, Amber, or Red clinician-facing flags.
P22 [Normal]: The system is explicitly non-diagnostic. Its purpose is to augment, not replace, professional judgment by supplying objective behavioral evidence that may not be visible through interview and observation alone. The thesis evaluated PsyClick using 105 normative adult participants and 15 clinical sessions. The software obtained an overall ISO/IEC 25010 mean of 4.54 out of 5.00, with Reliability rated 4.67 and Security rated 4.64. System-clinician agreement reached Cohen’s kappa of 0.466, classified as Moderate Agreement. These results support PsyClick as a technically functional prototype and a behavioral signal amplifier for controlled clinical and counseling settings.
P23 [Normal]: From an Information Assurance and Security perspective, PsyClick processes highly sensitive behavioral and mental-health-related data. Keystroke and cursor dynamics may function as behavioral biometrics; therefore, confidentiality, integrity, availability, authentication, authorization, accountability, privacy, and non-repudiation must be addressed throughout the system life cycle. The proposed security design uses local processing, data minimization, consent-gated capture, role-based access control, encrypted storage and backups, secure authentication, tamper-evident audit logging, integrity verification, and documented incident-response and recovery procedures.
P24 [Normal]: Document scope note: Sections describing the existing statistical pipeline and validated prototype are based on the PsyClick thesis. Security features not yet fully implemented in the research prototype are presented as the proposed CS0029 security hardening baseline and should be labeled “proposed” when inserted into the thesis.

P51 [Heading 1]: CHAPTER 3 – SYSTEM ANALYSIS AND DESIGN
P52 [Heading 2]: 3.1 System Requirements
P53 [Normal]: The system requirements are derived from the thesis workflow and the security obligations of handling behavioral biometrics and mental-health-related information. The primary user is the mental health professional, who authenticates, performs patient intake, confirms consent, administers calibration and assessment tasks, reviews results, and exports reports. The adult patient provides behavioral telemetry during the controlled tasks. A normative tester may complete the same protocol for research baseline development. An administrator manages accounts, policies, backups, and security configuration. An auditor or data-protection officer may review logs without changing clinical content.

P56 [Heading 2]: 3.2 Feasibility Study
P57 [Heading 3]: 3.2.1 Technical Feasibility
P58 [Normal]: PsyClick is technically feasible because the thesis demonstrates a complete local pipeline for telemetry capture, hardware normalization, feature extraction, EWMA baseline construction, Hotelling’s T² analysis, feature contribution, fuzzy classification, and report generation. The fixed eight-feature model has constant-size state and linear processing time over session windows, allowing operation on ordinary clinic or school-counseling workstations. SQLite is appropriate for a standalone prototype because it provides ACID transactions, file portability, foreign keys, and low administrative overhead. Security hardening requires a maintained cryptographic library, protected key storage, and disciplined application-layer authorization.
P59 [Heading 3]: 3.2.2 Operational Feasibility
P60 [Normal]: The workflow fits a controlled 10- to 15-minute counseling or assessment session. Consent is obtained before capture; patients complete familiar keyboard and mouse tasks; clinicians review a dashboard after their independent observation. The local-first model does not require continuous internet connectivity and reduces infrastructure complexity. Training is required to explain the meaning of T², PSI, PAI, fuzzy confidence, and the non-diagnostic Green/Amber/Red flags. Operational procedures must also define account provisioning, log review, incident reporting, and backup responsibilities.
P61 [Heading 3]: 3.2.3 Economic Feasibility
P62 [Normal]: The solution uses existing desktop computers, keyboards, and mice, avoiding specialized sensors or cloud subscriptions. Primary costs include development, secure configuration, staff training, encrypted backup media, maintenance, endpoint protection, and periodic security testing. For educational or research deployment, the local architecture offers a favorable cost profile because it avoids recurring hosting and network-transmission expenses.

P85 [Heading 2]: 3.7 Security Architecture
P86 [Normal]: Security follows defense in depth and privacy by design. The following layered architecture is recommended:
P87 [List Number]: Presentation Layer: hides administrative functions from non-administrators, masks sensitive fields, warns before exports, and locks the session after inactivity.
P88 [List Number]: Authentication Layer: validates unique user accounts, Argon2id/bcrypt password hashes, optional TOTP MFA, account status, lockout state, and secure session tokens.
P89 [List Number]: Authorization Layer: enforces RBAC and record ownership at every service method; UI visibility is supplementary and not the sole control.
P90 [List Number]: Consent and Privacy Layer: blocks telemetry capture until valid consent exists; supports withdrawal; minimizes fields and prohibits semantic analysis of typed content.
P91 [List Number]: Application Security Layer: validates inputs, uses parameterized SQL, catches exceptions safely, prevents path traversal, and applies secure defaults.
P92 [List Number]: Cryptographic Layer: encrypts protected database fields or the database file, exported reports, and backups; stores keys outside the database using OS-protected storage.
P93 [List Number]: Integrity and Audit Layer: uses transaction controls, report hashes, backup hashes, and chained audit entries; monitors failed login and mass-export patterns.
P94 [List Number]: Availability Layer: automatic backups, restore drills, transaction rollback, disk-space monitoring, controlled shutdown, and documented recovery objectives.
P95 [List Number]: Endpoint and Physical Layer: patched OS, endpoint protection, encrypted disk, restricted workstation access, automatic screen lock, and secure backup storage.

P117 [Heading 2]: 4.4 Encryption
P118 [Heading 3]: 4.4.1 Data at Rest
P119 [Normal]: The preferred design encrypts the application database and backup packages using AES-256 authenticated encryption. If full-database encryption is unavailable, restricted fields such as patient identity, clinical instrument values, and report paths are encrypted individually with AES-256-GCM. Authenticated encryption protects both confidentiality and integrity. The encryption key is not stored in the database; it is protected by the operating system credential manager, DPAPI, Keyring, or a separately permissioned key file.
P120 [Heading 3]: 4.4.2 Password Storage
P121 [Normal]: Passwords are never encrypted reversibly. They are hashed using Argon2id, preferably with memory, iteration, and parallelism parameters appropriate for the deployment workstation, or bcrypt with a work factor of at least 12 when Argon2id is unavailable. Each password receives a unique random salt. A server-side pepper may be stored separately in protected OS storage.
P122 [Heading 3]: 4.4.3 Data in Transit and Export
P123 [Normal]: The normal prototype performs analysis locally and therefore avoids network transmission. If a report or backup is transferred to institutional storage, the transfer must use a protected channel such as TLS 1.2 or later, SFTP, or an encrypted removable medium. Exported PDF/HTML files should be encrypted or placed in an encrypted container when they contain restricted information. Emailing unencrypted reports is prohibited.
P124 [Heading 3]: 4.4.4 Key Management
P125 [List Bullet]: Generate keys using a cryptographically secure random number generator.
P126 [List Bullet]: Assign a key identifier and creation date; do not expose the key in logs or user interfaces.
P127 [List Bullet]: Back up recovery keys separately under dual control.
P128 [List Bullet]: Rotate keys after compromise, personnel change, or scheduled policy interval.
P129 [List Bullet]: Re-encrypt protected records or maintain controlled access to historical keys during rotation.
P130 [List Bullet]: Destroy retired keys only after retention and recovery obligations are satisfied.

P161 [Heading 2]: 5.5 Security Analysis
P162 [Normal]: The strongest existing security properties of PsyClick are its local-first architecture, consent enforcement, transient within-session baseline, and non-semantic telemetry design. The thesis security evaluation reported a mean of 4.64/5.00, with strong ratings for consent gate enforcement, database stability, baseline discard, audit log integrity, and fine-grained access control. Local processing reduces exposure to cloud interception and centralized breach. The white-box statistical pipeline also supports integrity and accountability because every clinical flag is traceable to deterministic stages.
P163 [Normal]: The primary residual risks are endpoint compromise, weak credential management, unprotected database or export files, inadequate audit visibility, backup failure, and automation bias. The CS0029 hardening plan addresses these through encryption, MFA, stronger audit-chain design, formal backup testing, documented incident response, security-focused negative testing, and clearer role separation. Clinical risk remains even after technical controls; therefore, the output must always be presented as supportive evidence rather than a diagnosis.

P167 [Heading 1]: CHAPTER 6 – CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
P168 [Heading 2]: 6.1 Conclusions
P169 [Normal]: PsyClick demonstrates that a local, hardware-abstracted clinical decision support system can transform ordinary keyboard and mouse interactions into explainable within-session psychomotor indicators. The prototype successfully integrates HAL normalization, sliding-window filtering, EWMA baseline construction, Hotelling’s T² anomaly detection, PSI/PAI decomposition, fuzzy logic, and clinician-facing reporting. The thesis results show strong technical quality, crash-free operation across the normative sessions, excellent ISO/IEC 25010 ratings, and moderate agreement with independent clinician observation.
P170 [Normal]: The system’s security posture benefits from data minimization, local processing, consent-gated capture, transient baseline state, and auditable deterministic analysis. However, production or institutional deployment requires formal authentication hardening, RBAC, encryption at rest and in backup, protected exports, tamper-evident audit logs, routine vulnerability assessment, tested recovery, and documented incident response. With these controls, PsyClick can satisfy the core Information Assurance objectives of confidentiality, integrity, availability, authentication, authorization, accountability, and privacy while preserving its non-diagnostic clinical-support role.
